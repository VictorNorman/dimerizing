About this model

A hard-sphere gas in a box, with one addition: the reversible reaction 2A ⇌ B. Two A particles (blue) that collide may fuse into one B particle (red, drawn larger — twice the mass and twice the volume, so ∛2 ≈ 1.26 times the radius, and it gets hit more often), and a B that hits anything — another particle or a wall — may fall back apart into two A's. dimerization‑chance and dissociation‑chance set how likely each event is, per collision.

This is a demonstration of dynamic equilibrium: individual molecules keep reacting in both directions constantly, but the populations settle into a stable balance where the forward and reverse rates match. A + 2B never changes — only how the mass is distributed between the two species. If the reaction behaves like real mass-action chemistry, [B]/[A]² should hold roughly steady once equilibrium is reached, however you got there — try initial‑B = 0 versus starting with some B already present.

Collisions are exact hard-disk contacts, and they are resolved by solving for the instant the two disks touched, so what you see is exactly what collides. Ordinary collisions conserve momentum and kinetic energy exactly; only the reactive events exchange energy with an ambient bath at temperature (forming a bond releases the pair's relative kinetic energy, breaking one draws a thermal kick back in). Temperature and mass are in reduced units — temperature is the average kinetic energy per particle, with kB = 1 — not real kelvin and amu.
