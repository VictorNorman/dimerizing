About this model

A hard-sphere gas in a box, with one addition: the reversible reaction 2A ⇌ B. Two A particles (blue) that collide may fuse into one B particle (red, drawn larger — twice the mass and twice the volume, so ∛2 ≈ 1.26 times the radius, and it gets hit more often), and a B that hits anything — another particle or a wall — may fall back apart into two A's. dimerization probability and dissociation probability set how likely each event is, per collision.

This is a demonstration of dynamic equilibrium: individual molecules keep reacting in both directions constantly, but the populations settle into a stable balance where the forward and reverse rates match. A + 2B never changes — only how the mass is distributed between the two species. If the reaction behaves like real mass-action chemistry, [B]/[A]² should hold roughly steady once equilibrium is reached, however you got there — try initial number of B = 0 versus starting with some B already present.

Collisions are exact hard-disk contacts, resolved by solving for the instant the two disks touched, so what you see is exactly what collides. The gas is energy-closed: there is no bond energy and no thermal bath, so a reaction only moves kinetic energy around. A dimer carries away its pair's whole energy; a splitting dimer hands its energy to the two fragments at random. Momentum cannot also survive a two-body reaction — conserving it would pin the dimer's speed below the energy it must carry — so this model keeps the energy and lets the momentum go.

That makes temperature a result rather than a setting: the slider only says where it starts. Energy is fixed and T = E/N, so pairing A's up raises the temperature and splitting B's lowers it.

The box interior is 100 nm on a side at full operating area, which fixes every other unit: one patch is 1.27 nm, an A is 0.63 nm across, and one tick is 13.9 ps. Pressure is measured, not assumed — the gauge sums the momentum each wall absorbs, per species, giving partial pressures in mN/m that you can compare against N·kBT/A to see whether the ideal gas law holds in two dimensions.
